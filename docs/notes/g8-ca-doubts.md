# Grade 8 Creative Arts: doubts and decisions (for a Creative Arts teacher to check)

## Music (feat/g8-ca-music): 5 lessons, 14 questions each
- Scope follows the Grade 8 CAPS music plan. T1 literacy: note values (semibreve, dotted minim, minim, crotchet, quaver) and rests, 2/4 3/4 4/4, treble and bass clefs and letter names, the C and G major scales. T1 and T3 listening: the four families of instruments (one lesson, in T1). T2 literacy: triplet, F major, dynamics (piano, forte, crescendo, diminuendo), tempo (allegro, andante). T3 performing and creating: ostinato, lyrics, jingle, music technology. T4 literacy: 6/8, D major, moderato, presto, ritardando, a tempo, legato, staccato, and a listening write-up.
- Lessons teach about music; they do not ask for a performance, so no singing, playing or composing is marked. No song, score, lyric or recording is quoted: the only notes are scales and a four-note pattern (C E G E) made up for the lesson.
- Mnemonic and layout: the lines of the treble staff are given as E G B D F and the spaces as F A C E. The bass staff lines G B D F A and spaces A C E G are stated in words only (the picture shows the treble staff). Check these are the names your learners are taught.
- Triplet: taught as three triplet quavers in the time of one crotchet beat. A triplet crotchet or other triplets are not mentioned.
- Key signatures: G major F♯ (one sharp); F major B♭ (one flat); D major F♯ and C♯ (two sharps). Scales are drawn on the treble staff starting on the key note above middle C (C and D start on C4 and D4; G and F start on G4 and F4). The sharp or flat sign is drawn next to the note, not as a key signature at the start, so the picture shows the scale and not a piece of written music.
- 6/8: counted as two beats of three quavers (a dotted crotchet each). In slow or fast pieces some teachers count six; the lesson states the usual way.
- Tempo words are given with meanings only (andante at a walking pace, moderato at a moderate speed, allegro fast and lively, presto very fast). No metronome ranges are quoted, because books differ. The only tempo figure is 120 beats a minute in the jingle example.
- Instruments by family: membranophones (djembe, ngoma drum, snare drum), idiophones (marimba, triangle, shaker), chordophones (guitar, violin, uhadi), aerophones (flute, recorder, vuvuzela). The tambourine and the mbira were left out on purpose because they can be classed in more than one way. The uhadi is described as a musical bow with a string.
- Jingle: "about a minute long" is the CAPS length. The bar count (60 s at 120 beats a minute in 4/4 = 30 bars) is a worked example, not a rule.
- Four lines of lyrics: the plan (introduce the issue, say how it affects people, show what could change, end with a clear message) is a suggestion, not a CAPS rule. The topics named are bullying and littering.
- Not built: the term practical tasks (performance, composition, the formal assessments).

## Drama (feat/g8-ca-drama): 4 lessons, 14 questions each
- Scope follows the Grade 8 CAPS drama plan. T2: South African poetry or praise poetry (interpretation and presentation skills, and praise poetry features). T3: polished improvisation in a style with technical resources and a justification. T4: dialogues, dramatised prose or indigenous storytelling (CAPS says "or": one lesson covers all three so that nothing is missing, a class may do only one), and the Media topic. The warm-up routines, the T1 playmaking elements and the appreciation and reflection time are not built as lessons.
- No poem, praise poem, play, story or media programme is quoted or named. The praise phrases in the worked example ("Runner of the Long Field", "Keeper of the Open Gate") are made up for the lesson. The only real-world names are the terms imbongi (praise poet, isiXhosa and isiZulu) and ntsomi (isiXhosa folktale). Please check both terms and the sentence that a praise poet "welcomes and honours people and speaks about the life of the community": praise poetry differs between cultures, and the lesson keeps to the CAPS wording (repetition, descriptive naming phrases, rhythm, call and response, role in a community).
- Praise poetry is described as praising a person, group or place. The lesson says a praise poet may also speak about how people behave, which is a general description of the tradition; delete it if your teachers prefer to keep to the CAPS list.
- Styles are defined in one line each (comedy: humour and exaggeration; tragedy: serious, ends in loss or sorrow; musical: songs and dance tell the story; puppet show: puppets play the characters). The questions use the words usually or best where a style could overlap (a puppet show can also be funny).
- Technical resources: properties are things the actors hold or use, set pieces are scenery, costume pieces show who the character is, lighting shows time or mood, sound adds effects and music. In theatre books a "set piece" and a "prop" overlap for large objects; the questions use clear cases (a cardboard phone is a prop, a painted tree is a set piece).
- Status play: high status is tall posture, steady voice, taking space; low status is smaller, softer, looking down. The lesson says high status can be quiet and still.
- Media: film, television, radio, documentaries and the internet, with the drama elements what, who, where and when, tension and structure. Radio is described as sound only. No named programme or channel is used.
- Not built: the term practical tasks (performance, devised scenes and written reflections).

## Visual Arts (feat/g8-ca-visual): 7 lessons, 14 questions each
- Scope follows the Grade 8 CAPS visual arts plan. T1: creative lettering and graffiti (visual literacy and the ink design project); the clay pinch pot and the mythological container (with the functional containers literacy block). T2: fashion visual literacy with the 3D garment from recyclables and careers in fashion; societal issues in South African art with the pencil drawing. T3: careers in arts and design, surface decoration and 2D/3D construction. T4: crumpled-fabric tonal drawing, oil pastel and tempera artwork, wider-world visual literacy, and the scraperboard etching. Not built: the seated-model wet-media drawing (T3), the "describe own and others' artwork" literacy block (T3), and the practical projects themselves.
- Lessons teach about the projects and the vocabulary; no drawing, painting or making is marked. No artist or artwork is named; images are described generally (a poster, a mural, a dry tap).
- Complementary colours use the traditional red-yellow-blue wheel: red and green, blue and orange, yellow and purple. Some books use a different wheel (for example magenta and green); check this matches your teaching.
- Pencils: the lesson says a higher number of B is softer and makes a darker mark, and the B means black. Check your learners use HB, 2B, 3B and 4B.
- Clay: earthenware, pinch pot (ball, thumb, pinch and turn to raise even walls, smooth, decorate) and joining by scratching both surfaces and using slip. The lesson says uneven walls dry unevenly and can crack. Firing is not described. Please check the wording on joining, slip and drying against what your school does.
- Scraperboard (wax and ink): white wax crayon, black ink or black tempera with a little dishwashing liquid so that it sticks, dry completely, then scratch with a sharp tool. The lesson says more scratching gives a lighter tone, because white shows. Sharp tools (nails, pins, compass points) are the CAPS materials; the safety points (scratch away from your body, protect the table, never point a tool at a person, put tools away) are general practice, not CAPS text. Please check them.
- Graffiti: the lesson says to make graffiti on paper or with permission, and that making it on property without permission is vandalism. This is a general statement of the law and the school's values, written to match the CAPS values development; check it suits your school.
- Craft safety for the garment project: cut away from your body on a cutting surface; close craft knives; share tools. The garment example is a rain cape from plastic bags; the lesson does not give any instruction that needs an iron, a sewing machine or heat.
- Careers listed: graphic designer, illustrator, photographer, interior designer, art teacher, fashion designer, pattern maker, tailor or seamstress, stylist, textile designer. Descriptions are one line each.
- Symbols: a dove for peace, an empty plate for hunger, joined hands for unity, a tree in rubbish for hope. These are common examples, not the only ones; some learners may read them differently.
- Design principles: Term 1 lettering names contrast, proportion, emphasis, unity and balance; Term 4 drawing names balance, proportion, harmony, rhythm, emphasis and contrast. The one-line definitions are simple classroom wording.

## Dance (feat/g8-ca-dance): 2 lessons, 14 questions each
- Scope follows the Grade 8 CAPS dance plan. T3 theory and literacy: discussing a dance seen in the community, on television or on stage (type of dance, movements, type of music, ideas or themes), the dance elements space (symmetry, asymmetry, patterning), force and active and passive relationships, and dance and related careers research and presentation (skills, qualifications, qualities, description). T4 improvisation and composition: gestures from literal to abstract, combining gestures into a movement sentence, and composing a sequence about an idea, mood or thought, with reflection. The warm-up, floor work, steps, turns, jumps and the group dance are practical and are not built as lessons; the existing Dance lessons cover the other Grade 8 dance items.
- No dance, dancer, choreographer, show or piece is named. The traditional-dance example is described generally (a wedding dance with drums and singing); check that the example is appropriate for your learners' communities.
- Careers listed: dancer, choreographer, dance teacher, dance therapist, costume designer. Qualifications are described only as training and study; no school, course or entry requirement is given.
- Symmetry, asymmetry and force are defined in one line each. The lesson says active and passive describe a leading and a following dancer.
- Literal and abstract gestures: literal means the gesture shows an everyday action as it looks (wave, push, wipe, reach); abstract means it no longer looks like the everyday action but still shows the idea or feeling. The ways of changing a gesture (size, speed, body part, level, direction, repeating, stretching) are common composition teaching, not CAPS text.
- Movement sentence counts: 4 gestures x 2 counts = 8 counts; repeating the sentence twice gives 8 gestures in 16 counts. The question that asks which gesture could start a joyful sequence (arms opening wide and rising) is an example; other choices are possible.
- Not built: the term practical tasks (formal performance and composition) and the Term 4 theory revision.
