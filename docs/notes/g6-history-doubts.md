# Grade 6 History Wave A (Terms 1 and 2): points for a teacher to confirm (bron, 2026-11-17)

Source of scope: CAPS Social Sciences Grades 4-6, Grade 6 History Term 1 (Mapungubwe) and Term 2 (Explorers from Europe). Plan: audit/wave-a/bron-g6-history-plan.md. Every date is in `src/components/diagrams/history/history6.ts` and pinned by a test. The CAPS dates are exact; every other date is written "about". No photographs or drawings of real artefacts are used (no source to credit): the pictures are labelled schematics (timeline bars, a plan of a hill, tables, flow charts). Mapungubwe is told as an African achievement in its own right.

## Branch A: Mapungubwe (Term 1)

| # | What the lessons say | How sure | Where to check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mapungubwe was a state from AD 1220 to AD 1300 (80 years). | Exact, from the CAPS. | CAPS Gr 6 History Term 1 |
| 2 | Schroda about AD 900 to 1000; K2 about AD 1000 to 1220; the people of K2 moved to Mapungubwe Hill about AD 1220. | The order is the standard archaeological sequence; the dates are rounded textbook figures (hence "about"). | Textbook, Mapungubwe Archive (University of Pretoria) |
| 3 | At Schroda people farmed, kept cattle, made pottery, worked ivory and had glass beads that show trade with far-away people. | Standard; the lesson gives no counts. | Textbook |
| 4 | K2 was a bigger settlement where cattle were a sign of wealth and leaders became more important. | Standard textbook account. | Textbook |
| 5 | The king was a sacred leader and lived on top of the hill; the palace there was the first stone-walled palace; the first town was below the hill; there were distinct social classes shown by where people lived and by graves (gold on the hill). | CAPS wording; the "sacred powers" gloss is the usual textbook explanation. The plan of the hill is a schematic, not to scale, not a drawing of the real site. | CAPS; Textbook |
| 6 | The town had "farmers, herders, potters and metalworkers". | Standard (pottery, metalwork and gold work are all found). | Textbook |
| 7 | The golden rhinoceros, a golden bowl and a golden sceptre were found in royal graves on the hill in the 1930s. | The 1930s discovery is standard; the exact year (1933 or 1934) and the exact objects differ a little by source, so the lesson says only "in the 1930s" and "including". | Textbook; museum notes |
| 8 | Traded: gold and ivory out; glass beads and cloth in; routes on foot to the east coast and across the Indian Ocean ("globalisation"). | CAPS lists the goods only generally; these are the standard examples. The lesson names no port and no distant country. | Textbook |
| 9 | Dangers (wild animals, lack of water, getting lost) and ways of finding the way (landmarks, sun and stars, local guides). | General and reasonable, not from a source about a particular journey. | Teacher judgement |
| 10 | The Mapungubwe Cultural Landscape became a World Heritage Site in 2003; the Order of Mapungubwe was created in 2002. | Standard. | UNESCO list; the Presidency's National Orders |
| 11 | "more than 700 years ago": 2026 minus 1300 is 726. | Arithmetic; a test checks it. | |
| 12 | The meaning of the word "Mapungubwe" is NOT given. | Several glosses are quoted and none is certain. | |

## Branch B: East coast trade and Great Zimbabwe; Marco Polo (Term 1)

| # | What the lessons say | How sure | Where to check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 13 | Inland people traded gold and ivory with the east coast; merchants from across the Indian Ocean (Arabia, Persia, India) brought cloth and glass beads. | Standard; the CAPS says only "East Coast trade with settlements inland". No port, city or ruler is named. | Textbook |
| 14 | The trade continued before and after Mapungubwe (continuity); the centre of trade and power moved north to Great Zimbabwe (change). | The CAPS describes Great Zimbabwe as succeeding Mapungubwe as the centre of southern African trade. | CAPS Term 1 background |
| 15 | Great Zimbabwe is in present-day Zimbabwe and was most powerful from **about AD 1300 to 1450** (about 150 years). | The dates are rounded textbook figures: the site was settled earlier and the reasons for its decline are debated, so the lesson says "about" and gives no reason for the decline. | Textbook; Great Zimbabwe World Heritage listing |
| 16 | Built by the ancestors of the Shona people, with great stone walls laid without mortar; rulers wealthy from cattle and the gold trade; glass beads and porcelain show long-distance trade. | Standard and the accepted view of historians and archaeologists. The lesson also corrects the old colonial-era claim that foreigners built it, in one sentence. | Textbook |
| 17 | The meaning of the name "Great Zimbabwe" is NOT given. | Glosses vary; left out. | |
| 18 | Marco Polo: a merchant's son from Venice, left in 1271 with his father and uncle, came home in 1295 (24 years), travelled mostly overland, served the Mongol ruler Kublai Khan, and his travels were written in a book. | Standard. The lesson does not name the co-author of the book, the route or any city on the way. | Encyclopaedia, textbook |
| 19 | The book described silk, spices, porcelain and paper money, and was copied by hand because there was no printing press in Europe yet; it made European traders and explorers more interested in Asia (about 200 years before the voyages of Term 2). | Standard. "About 200 years" is a rounded gap between the 1290s and the 1480s. | Textbook |
| 20 | The lesson says that some Europeans found the book hard to believe but later explorers found much of it to be true. | A general statement; not tied to a named person. | Teacher judgement |
| 21 | Marco Polo's whole journey (1271 to 1295) lies inside the years of Mapungubwe (1220 to 1300), as the CAPS says ("at the same time as Mapungubwe was at its height"). | Arithmetic; a test checks it. | |
