# Grade 6 Mathematics (CAPS gap topics): facts to check (teacher review pack)

Source: CAPS Mathematics Grades 4-6, section 3.3.3 "Clarification of content for Grade 6". Nothing here was left out of a bank because of doubt; where the CAPS text itself looks wrong, the lessons avoid it and the reason is recorded here.

## Adding and subtracting whole numbers (Terms 1 and 3)

- **CAPS example arithmetic that is wrong.** The Term 1 "Checking solutions" notes say "45 362 + 32 488 = 77 848" and "54 687 - 32 134 = 22 544". The true values are 45 362 + 32 488 = **77 850** and 54 687 - 32 134 = **22 553**. The lessons and questions use the correct numbers. The CAPS worked examples 56 423 + 7 581 + 21 479 = 85 483 and 98 743 - 45 684 = 53 059 are right and are used as they stand.
- **Rounding for estimates.** CAPS says learners can round 4-digit numbers to the nearest 1 000 and 5-digit numbers to the nearest 10 000 (or continue with 1 000). The 5-digit lesson uses 1 000 for 45 678 + 12 345 (the CAPS example); the 6-digit lesson uses 10 000 and 100 000. Halves round up (45 500 rounds to 46 000).
- **Problem types and numbers.** The six-digit problem questions (DVD players, pears, sugar, factory income, farm price) are the CAPS "Problem types" examples with the same numbers; all answers were recomputed. The shop in the Check yourself slide is an invented example.
- **Compensating** (4 999 + 3 245 = 5 000 + 3 245 - 1) is the "rounding off and compensating" technique in the CAPS list, taught with a simple example.

## Multiplication and division of whole numbers (Terms 2 and 4)

- **CAPS example arithmetic that is wrong.** The long-division example in the Term 2 notes reads "Calculate 3 848 ÷ 132 ... 26 remainder 52" with the steps 132 × 20 = 2 640 and 132 × 6 = 792. Those steps only work for **3 484**: 132 × 26 + 52 = 3 484 (3 848 ÷ 132 is 29 remainder 20). The lessons and questions use 3 484 ÷ 132 = 26 remainder 52.
- **CAPS examples that check out and are used as they stand:** 547 × 45 = 24 615 (by 547 × 40 + 547 × 5 and by 547 × 50 − 547 × 5), 547 × 42 = 22 974 (by the factors 6 × 7), 4 362 × 108 = 471 096 (8 × 4 362 = 34 896 and 100 × 4 362 = 436 200), the four estimates of 4 362 × 108 (400 000, 432 000, 436 200, 475 200) and 3 447 ÷ 17 = 202 remainder 13 (200 × 17 = 3 400 and 2 × 17 = 34).
- **Remainders in problems.** The buses question (300 learners, 24 seats) teaches that a remainder of learners needs one more bus (13 buses). This "round up" reading is standard school practice; the CAPS text only lists "grouping and equal sharing with remainders" as a problem type.
- **Number format.** Every formatted number uses a non-breaking space between groups of three digits (grade6AddSub.group, nb), so a number never splits across two lines. Questions, options and explanations are tested for it.

## Percentages (Term 3)

- **Scope.** CAPS says percentages are new in Grade 6 and limits them to tenths and hundredths, fractions whose denominators are factors of 100, decimals in tenths and hundredths, and a percentage of a whole number ("learners are not expected to convert any common fraction into its percentage form"). The lesson and the 14 questions use only 2, 4, 5, 10, 20, 25 and 50 and 100 as denominators, and the lesson test fails if 1/3 or 1/8 appears as something to convert. The CAPS example 25% of R300 = 1/4 of R300 = R75 is used as it stands (the CAPS text writes "25% of R30 = 1/4 of R300" with a typing slip; the correct statement is used).
- **Discount** (a R450 jacket at 10% off costs R405) is a simple financial application of "percentage of a whole number"; CAPS does not mention discounts for Grade 6, so a teacher should confirm it is welcome in Term 3.

## Temperature (Term 3)

- **Referents.** 0 °C, 37 °C and 100 °C are the CAPS referents. "A hot summer day is about 33 °C" and "a cold winter morning about 3 °C" are everyday estimates, not CAPS statements, offered as sensible-guess examples.
- **Scales.** The thermometer pictures are drawn from the numbers in the lesson (0 to 40 with a line every 2 degrees; digital readings with tenths such as 36,7 °C). Temperatures are positive whole numbers or one decimal place only, as in CAPS ("limited to positive whole numbers and decimal fractions").

## Probability experiments and position and movement (Term 4)

- **Probability scope.** CAPS Grade 6 only asks for listing outcomes, recording with tally marks and counting and comparing frequencies (up to 50 trials, collating group results). The lesson never writes a probability as a fraction and a test fails on "chance", "likely" or "probability of". The sentence that more trials make heads and tails come out closer is from the CAPS note ("compare the number of outcomes that occur as the number of trials increase") and is kept qualitative.
- **Tally and grid conventions.** Tally groups of five have the fifth stroke across the other four. On the grid, letters run across the top, numbers run down the side, rows are counted from the top, and the grid reference is letter first (CAPS examples D4, A3, E7). The questions say this in their text because question items carry no picture.
- **Compass.** The compass questions say north is at the top of the map, so up is north, right is east. CAPS leaves the compass work to Geography ("practised in Mathematics").

## Transformations (Terms 3 and 4)

- **Describe, not draw.** CAPS Grade 6 Term 3 only asks learners to use translation, reflection, rotation and symmetry to describe patterns (honeycomb = a translated hexagon, bead bracelet = a reflected triangle, doily = a translated parallelogram), and says they are no longer required to draw tessellations. The lesson and questions only name and describe; the grid questions give the new position of a point.
- **"Bees do not tessellate".** CAPS notes that the geometrical process used to copy a pattern is not the one that made it. The lesson's common mistake on the describing-patterns slide says so.
- **Enlargement ratio.** CAPS says "increasing or decreasing the lengths of all sides by the same ratio, e.g. doubling". The lesson uses whole-number multipliers (doubling, tripling) and halving only, so every length stays a whole number. The "area becomes 4 times as big" link follows the CAPS note that this "links with work done on area".
- **Mirror line in the questions.** A mirror line "down the middle of column D" or "along the edge between row 3 and row 4" is described in words because items carry no picture; the keys use the cell as a point.

## Viewing objects (Term 3)

- **Scope.** CAPS Grade 6 extends Grade 5 views of everyday objects to geometric objects, collections of geometric objects and composite geometric objects, matching each view with the position of the viewer. The lesson uses towers of unit cubes (composite geometric objects) plus a cylinder, a cone and a cube (everyday objects such as a tin, a party hat and a dice).
- **Convention for the questions.** Items carry no picture, so a structure is described in words: tower heights from left to right as seen from the front, front row first. The back view reverses the order. From the right-hand side the viewer sees the front row on their left; from the left-hand side the back row is on their left. Each view lists the tallest tower in each line of sight. A teacher may want to check that this matches the textbook convention used in the learner's school.
- **Top view.** The lesson's top view shows how many squares are covered and writes each tower's height on its square, with the back row drawn at the top of the view.
