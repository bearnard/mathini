# Grade 11 Physical Sciences: doubts

## Term 1: gravitation, atomic combinations, water
- Constants: G = 6,67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²·kg⁻² and the Earth's mass and radius are the NSC data-sheet values; every question that needs them states them. The Moon's mass (7,35 × 10²² kg) and radius (1,74 × 10⁶ m) are NOT on the data sheet; they are given in the questions and are common reference values, rounded.
- g from the data-sheet values is 9,80 m·s⁻² (9,799), not the usual 9,8 exactly; the lessons use 9,80 for weight sums. If the school marks with 9,8, the sums differ in the third figure only.
- Bond polarity rule: ΔEN up to 0,4 non-polar covalent, above 0,4 up to 1,7 polar covalent, above 1,7 ionic. This is a common rule of thumb, but the CAPS text does not fix these numbers, and textbooks differ (some use 0,5 or 2,0). It is stated in the lesson as "the rule in this lesson". By this rule C–H (0,4) is non-polar. Please check against the textbook the teacher uses. H–F (ΔEN 1,9) is deliberately NOT used because the rule would call it ionic while it is a polar covalent bond.
- Pauling electronegativities (H 2,1; C 2,5; N 3,0; O 3,5; F 4,0; Cl 3,0; Na 0,9) are the usual data-sheet values.
- VSEPR: the lesson stops at four electron pairs (linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal pyramidal, bent). Bond angles 107° (NH₃) and 104,5° (H₂O) are the standard textbook values. Tetrahedral shapes are drawn flat in the picture, with their angle in a label.
- Molecular polarity: the net-dipole test adds the bond dipoles as vectors in the true 3D shape (tested). CO₂, BF₃ and CH₄ cancel; NH₃ and H₂O do not. BF₃ is in the picture only through a question about symmetrical shapes.
- Bond length and energy: only the trend (single longer and weaker than double, triple shortest and strongest). No bond-energy figures are given because textbooks differ.
- Water: boiling points of H₂S (−60 °C), H₂Se (−41 °C) and H₂Te (−2 °C) are rounded textbook values. "Water has the smallest mass of the four" is correct (18 g·mol⁻¹). Density: 1,000 g·cm⁻³ at 4 °C (exactly 0,99997), 0,9998 at 0 °C, 0,9997 at 10 °C, 0,9982 at 20 °C, ice 0,917 g·cm⁻³; 1 000 g of ice takes 1 091 cm³ (about 9% more). The lesson says water is densest at 4 °C, which is the standard CAPS statement.
- "Up to four hydrogen bonds per water molecule" is the standard textbook limit (two through the hydrogens, two through the lone pairs).
- Not taught here: specific heat capacity and surface tension of water (not named in the CAPS water topic as I read it).
