# Grade 11 Physical Sciences: doubts

## Term 1: gravitation, atomic combinations, water
- Constants: G = 6,67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²·kg⁻² and the Earth's mass and radius are the NSC data-sheet values; every question that needs them states them. The Moon's mass (7,35 × 10²² kg) and radius (1,74 × 10⁶ m) are NOT on the data sheet; they are given in the questions and are common reference values, rounded.
- g from the data-sheet values is 9,80 m·s⁻² (9,799), not the usual 9,8 exactly; the lessons use 9,80 for weight sums. If the school marks with 9,8, the sums differ in the third figure only.
- Bond polarity rule: ΔEN up to 0,4 non-polar covalent, above 0,4 up to 1,7 polar covalent, above 1,7 ionic. This is a common rule of thumb, but the CAPS text does not fix these numbers, and textbooks differ (some use 0,5 or 2,0). It is stated in the lesson as "the rule in this lesson". By this rule C–H (0,4) is non-polar. Please check against the textbook the teacher uses. H–F (ΔEN 1,9) is deliberately NOT used because the rule would call it ionic while it is a polar covalent bond.
- Pauling electronegativities (H 2,1; C 2,5; N 3,0; O 3,5; F 4,0; Cl 3,0; Na 0,9) are the usual data-sheet values.
- VSEPR: the lesson stops at four electron pairs (linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal pyramidal, bent). Bond angles 107° (NH₃) and 104,5° (H₂O) are the standard textbook values. Tetrahedral shapes are drawn flat in the picture, with their angle in a label.
- Molecular polarity: the net-dipole test adds the bond dipoles as vectors in the true 3D shape (tested). CO₂, BF₃ and CH₄ cancel; NH₃ and H₂O do not. BF₃ is in the picture only through a question about symmetrical shapes.
- Bond length and energy: only the trend (single longer and weaker than double, triple shortest and strongest). No bond-energy figures are given because textbooks differ.
- Water: boiling points of H₂S (−60 °C), H₂Se (−41 °C) and H₂Te (−2 °C) are rounded textbook values. "Water has the smallest mass of the four" is correct (18 g·mol⁻¹). Density: 1,000 g·cm⁻³ at 4 °C (exactly 0,99997), 0,9998 at 0 °C, 0,9997 at 10 °C, 0,9982 at 20 °C, ice 0,917 g·cm⁻³; 1 000 g of ice takes 1 091 cm³ (about 9% more). The lesson says water is densest at 4 °C, which is the standard CAPS statement.
- "Up to four hydrogen bonds per water molecule" is the standard textbook limit (two through the hydrogens, two through the lone pairs).
- Not taught here: specific heat capacity and surface tension of water (not named in the CAPS water topic as I read it).

## Term 2 optics: refraction and Snell's law, wavefronts and diffraction
- Refractive indices used (air 1,00; water 1,33; glass 1,50; diamond 2,42) are the usual textbook values; the data sheet gives none except c = 3,00 × 10⁸ m·s⁻¹. Every question that needs an index states it in the question. Glass varies from about 1,5 to 1,9, so "glass 1,50" is stated as an example.
- Critical angles are given to one decimal (glass-air 41,8°, water-air 48,8°, diamond-air 24,4°, core 1,50 to cladding 1,45 75,2°). Answers recomputed with plain Math in the tests; each wrong option is a different real angle.
- The fibre example uses made-up but realistic core and cladding indices (1,50 and 1,45).
- Diffraction is qualitative in CAPS. The lesson uses "degree of diffraction increases with λ/a" (CAPS: degree of diffraction ∝ λ). The first dark places at sin θ = λ/a for a single slit are in the lesson's last worked example and in the picture, which goes slightly beyond the CAPS (it is not asked in a question). Please say if the lesson should stay purely qualitative.
- The intensity curve (sin β / β)² is the standard single-slit result, drawn for 600 nm light through a 3,0 µm slit; it is a picture only.
- The classroom examples (sound through a 0,9 m doorway, red light versus blue) are illustrations, not measurements.
- Not covered: the practical project (verifying Snell's law with a glass block) and sketching diffraction patterns for learners to draw.

## Term 2 chemistry: ideal gases, moles and concentration, stoichiometry
- Three lessons instead of the planned two: the CAPS "quantitative aspects" topic is 12 hours (molar volume and concentration 3 h, "more complex stoichiometric calculations" 6 h), so it is split into "Moles, Gas Volumes and Concentration" and "Stoichiometry" for depth. All the numbers are recomputed with plain Math in `src/g11PsGases.test.ts`.
- Data-sheet constants quoted in the questions: R = 8,31 J·K⁻¹·mol⁻¹, molar volume 22,4 dm³·mol⁻¹ at 273 K and 101,3 kPa (the lessons say 0 °C or 273 K), Cl = 35,5, T = °C + 273. Avogadro's number is 6,02 × 10²³. Atomic masses used: H 1, C 12, N 14, O 16, Na 23, Mg 24, Cl 35,5, Ca 40 (the data-sheet values).
- Kinetic theory is stated in the form of the CAPS: random motion, different speeds, collisions with the walls, average speed rising with temperature, real gases closest to ideal at high temperature and low pressure. No Maxwell-Boltzmann curve is drawn.
- Gay-Lussac's law (constant volume) is named in the CAPS and appears in the lesson and one question.
- Empirical and molecular formula examples (a C₄H₈ hydrocarbon 85,7% C and 14,3% H; glucose 40,0% C, 6,7% H, 53,3% O) are standard textbook examples with rounded percentages: the tests check that the ratio is within 0,05 of a whole number.
- The stoichiometry examples are small made-up experiments (6,0 g Mg; 4,0 g H₂ with 8,0 g O₂; a 10,0 g shell sample losing 3,52 g of CO₂ on heating, which is exactly 0,08 mol). Percent purity by heating is a simplification of the CAPS sea-shell activity.
- Not covered here: the practical tasks (verifying Boyle's law, making standard solutions, titration and precipitation calculations). Titrations are Gr 12 in our lessons; precipitation calculations can be added later.
- Real-gas deviations are described qualitatively only; the van der Waals equation is not in the CAPS and is not used.

## Term 3: electromagnetism, energy and chemical change, acid-base reactions
- Electromagnetism: CAPS Gr 11 states Faraday's law and the flux Φ = BA cos θ qualitatively, with the Right Hand Rule for directions. The lesson gives the proportionality ("a faster change in flux gives a bigger emf") and the flux calculation, but NOT the formula emf = −N ΔΦ/Δt, which I read as Grade 12 work. θ is the angle to the NORMAL, as in the CAPS. If the teacher uses the angle to the surface, cos and sin swap.
- Right Hand Rule: thumb along the current, fingers show the field (wire); fingers follow the current, thumb points to N (coil). Lenz's law is taught as "the near end of the solenoid takes the same pole when a pole is pushed in and the opposite when pulled out". Pictures and questions are tested for the four cases.
- Overhead cables: CAPS asks for a qualitative discussion of environmental impact. The lesson says they have magnetic fields, take up land and can harm birds, and that people study the effects on health. It makes no health claim.
- Energy and chemical change: values 50, 90, 20 and 20, 80, 50 kJ·mol⁻¹ are made up for the graphs. ΔH = products − reactants; Ea(forward) − Ea(reverse) = ΔH is tested. No bond-energy calculation is given (CAPS Gr 11 relates energy changes to bond energy qualitatively).
- Photosynthesis is classed as endothermic and respiration and combustion as exothermic, as the CAPS asks.
- Acid-base: Arrhenius and Brønsted-Lowry definitions, conjugate pairs and ampholytes (water). All five equations are balanced by an atom count in tests. The lesson stays qualitative on titration (CAPS: "leave until Grade 12 or do a simple qualitative titration"); no pH or concentration calculations. Strong and weak acids, Ka and Kb and pH are Grade 12 and are not taught.
- CAPS also lists producing and isolating salts (Na₂SO₄, CuSO₄, CaCO₃) and the use of lime and ash in pit toilets. The lesson mentions evaporating to get the salt but not the pit-toilet activity or CaCO₃ by precipitation; a teacher may want to add them.
- The lesson reuses the Grade 12 conjugate-pair and titration pictures.
